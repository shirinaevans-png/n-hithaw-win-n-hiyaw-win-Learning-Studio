nîhithawîwin · Woodland Cree (TH)

An introduction to Woodland Cree writing, structure, community and linguistic learning.

Where Woodland Cree is spoken

nîhithawîwin is commonly identified as Woodland Cree, Woods Cree or TH-dialect Cree. It is associated with communities in northern Saskatchewan and Manitoba. Solomon Ratt’s materials foreground the Woods Cree speech of Stanley Mission (āmaciwīspimowinihk). The region, its communities and its linguistic relationships cannot be represented by an administrative boundary alone.

Sources: Solomon Ratt, mâci-nîhithawîwin: Beginning Woods Cree (2026), Introduction, pp. 3–4; Ratt, Woods Cree Stories (2014), Notes on the Text.

Sounds, writing and syllabics

Ratt presents Woodland Cree in a Roman writing system, SROw. In his introductory account the vowels are a, â, i, î, o and ô, and the distinctive th sequence is important. Syllabics provide another writing system. Both Roman orthography and syllabics have spelling conventions: they are not simply visual substitutions for English letters.

Grammar is more than vocabulary

Cree nouns distinguish animate and inanimate grammatical classes. Verb structure also varies depending on whether a subject is animate or inanimate, whether a verb takes an object, and the object’s grammatical animacy. The verb families used throughout this studio are VAI, VII, VTI and VTA. Personal prefixes, verb stems and endings interact with person, number, mood and direct/inverse relationships.

Word building is meaningful only when the analysis corresponds to an attested morphological pattern. A complete dictionary entry is not necessarily a root or stem.

Ratt (2026), chapters 2, 4, 5, 7 and 8.

Language, land and storytelling

Ratt’s Woods Cree Stories treats stories as a means of passing on cultural learning, including respect, patience, names, relationships and collective responsibility. This studio approaches Cree words as parts of communicative and cultural systems, rather than isolated translations.

The book’s full stories and illustrations are not reproduced here. Learners can consult the published books and community-led resources for deeper study.

Ratt, Woods Cree Stories (University of Regina Press, 2014), Introduction, pp. ix–xiii.

About the Cree language map

Dialect regions vary and overlap. The supplied map shows Eeyou Istchee in Québec, an Eastern Cree territory. It is not a map of Woodland Cree territory, and the Cree varieties of Eeyou Istchee should not be relabelled Woodland Cree (TH).
