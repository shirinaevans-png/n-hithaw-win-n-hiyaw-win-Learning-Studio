1. Whose hand?

Some Cree body-part terms are bound to a possessor. Compare the forms for my, your, and his/her hand. The dictionary form micihciy uses mi- where a possessive element can appear.

6. Four ways to study word structure

Morphology

How meaningful pieces are combined to build words. Compare nicihciy, kicihciy, and ocihciy.

Lexical categories

How a word functions as a noun or verb. Compare wîpicihciy and wîpicihcê.

Inflection

How forms change with grammatical person and participants. Compare the hand-washing examples above.

Semantics

How a recurring element contributes meaning. Compare kâsîcihcê(k), kâsîhkwê(k), and kâsîhiyâkanê.

The lesson focuses on these four fields. Phonology, syntax, and discourse are further branches of linguistics explored elsewhere in the Learning Studio.

9. More word families: nouns, verbs and inflection

Explore actual examples from published linguistic analyses of Plains Cree. Each card aligns a Cree word with its component meanings. Select a form to see how its grammatical structure changes.

Possession and diminutives: shoes and little shoes

Some nouns mark a possessor in more than one place in the word. A diminutive can appear between the noun stem and possession and plural endings.

Three ways of seeing: verb classes

Cree verb stems distinguish the kinds of participants involved. The English verb “see” therefore corresponds to different Cree stems, not one interchangeable root.

Independent, conjunct and conditional

Compare how a ‘see’ verb changes across grammatical orders. The examples use the same verb class, with different combinations of prefixes and endings.

Direct and inverse: who sees whom?

Both participants matter. Direction marking helps show whether a first person acts on a third person, or a third person acts on a first person.
