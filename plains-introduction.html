nêhiyawêwin · Plains Cree (Y)

An introduction to Plains Cree language, writing, word formation and community-led lexicography.

Where Plains Cree is spoken

nêhiyawêwin is the Y-dialect commonly called Plains Cree. It is spoken across communities in Saskatchewan and Alberta, with local ways of speaking and orthographic preferences. One well-documented variety is spoken in Maskwacîs, Alberta; it should not be taken as the only form of Plains Cree.

Sources: Ratt, mâci-nîhithawîwin (2026), p. 3, for dialect comparison; Arppe et al., “nêhiyawi-pîkiskwêwina maskwacîsihk” (2023), pp. 127–130.

Writing and phonology

Plains Cree uses Standard Roman Orthography (SRO) alongside Cree syllabics. SRO distinguishes vowel length; one common spelling convention uses a, â, i, î, o, ô and ê. These spellings are not interchangeable with Woodland Cree spellings on a one-to-one letter basis, and some community conventions differ.

The syllabics section of this studio currently uses a Woodland Cree chart. It should not be presented as a fully localized Plains Cree syllabics course without adding the correct Plains-specific materials.

Understanding word structure

Plains Cree is polysynthetic: a single inflected verb can encode grammatical information that English often expresses in multiple words. Four important verb classes are VAI (animate intransitive), VII (inanimate intransitive), VTI (transitive inanimate) and VTA (transitive animate). Direct/inverse forms and obviation matter especially in VTA patterns.

The word builder uses documented example paradigms rather than applying a single set of endings to all roots. Select a word, inspect its class, and compare forms with the grammar information for that entry.

Community-led lexicography and recordings

The Maskwacîs Spoken Dictionary project brought together community speakers and linguistic researchers to record words and sentences, review pronunciations and definitions, and develop a web-accessible Cree lexicon. The published research describes roughly 20,300 word and phrase types documented through thousands of recordings.

The project also links computational analysis of word structures with dictionary lookup, showing how an inflected form can connect to a dictionary entry. For this studio, those research methods are examples to learn from, not a claim that its full audio archive or dictionary has been imported.

Other Cree language varieties

The illustrated dictionary and animal cards you provided feature distinct Cree language and regional traditions. Where those sources represent Eastern Cree, their labels and syllabics belong to that variety and should not be substituted for Plains Cree or Woodland Cree vocabulary without clear comparative documentation. Animal names, species illustrations and dialect variants can be linked in a future comparative catalogue with separate provenance and usage rights.
